\chapter{Spectral Shape Analysis}\label{ch:spectral}

Spectral shape analysis studies a shape through the eigenvalues and eigenfunctions of its
Laplace--Beltrami operator. Because the operator depends only on the metric, everything built from
its spectrum is invariant to isometric deformations: a cat is described in the same way whether it
stands, sits or licks its paw. The eigenfunctions also form a Fourier basis on the surface, which
turns the heat and wave equations into simple filters and leads to descriptors (ShapeDNA, the heat
and wave kernel signatures), distances (diffusion, commute-time, biharmonic) and canonical
embeddings. This chapter follows Tema 13 and the companion notebook, which uses the cats and horses
of the TOSCA collection in several poses. From now on we use the \emph{positive} Laplacian
$L=-\Div\grad$, discretised by $\mL=-\mC$, and the generalized eigenproblem
\begin{equation}
\mL\bphi_k=\lambda_k\mM\bphi_k,\qquad 0=\lambda_1<\lambda_2\le\dots,\qquad \mPhi\T\mM\mPhi=\mI ,
\label{eq:spec}
\end{equation}
on meshes rescaled to unit area. Eigenfunctions are indexed from $\phi_1$ (constant) as in the
figures.

\section{Why spectral analysis}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Intrinsic}: the spectrum is invariant to rigid motions and to isometric bending, the
  deformations of articulated and organic shapes.
  \item \textbf{Multiscale}: low frequencies describe the global structure, high frequencies the
  details, and filters in the spectral domain select the scale.
  \item \textbf{Efficient}: a few hundred eigenpairs, computed once with a sparse eigensolver, are
  enough for most tasks.
  \item \textbf{Physical}: heat diffusion and wave propagation have closed-form solutions in the
  eigenbasis, which gives every construction a physical interpretation.
\end{itemize}

\section{Calculus on manifolds}

Recall from \cref{ch:diffgeo,ch:smooth} that on a Riemannian manifold $\M$ with metric $g$, the
gradient $\grad f$ of a function is the tangent vector field with $\dd f(\bw)=g(\grad f,\bw)$, the
divergence of a tangent field $\bm X$ measures its net flow out of an infinitesimal region
($\int_\Omega\Div\bm X=\oint_{\partial\Omega}\inner{\bm X}{\bn}$), and the Laplace--Beltrami operator is
$\Lap f=\Div\grad f$. With the positive sign convention $L=-\Lap$, Green's identity reads
$\int_\M gLf\dd A=\int_\M\inner{\grad f}{\grad g}\dd A$, so $L$ is symmetric and positive
semi-definite. On a graph, the analogous operator is the graph Laplacian $\mL=\mD-\mA$ (degree minus
adjacency); on a triangle mesh, the cotangent stiffness matrix of \eqref{eq:cotan-matrix} with the sign
flipped, together with the mass matrix $\mM$.

\paragraph{Properties and the absence of a free lunch.} A good discrete Laplacian should be
symmetric, local, have linear precision (vanish on linear functions in flat regions), have positive
weights (a maximum principle), and converge to the smooth operator under refinement.
\citet{Wardetzky2007} showed that no discrete Laplacian has all of these properties on general
meshes. The cotangent Laplacian satisfies all but positivity, which fails on non-Delaunay edges;
since it converges and has linear precision, it is the standard choice for spectral analysis.

\section{Physics: heat and waves}

The \emph{heat equation} $\partial_tu=-Lu$ describes diffusion, and the \emph{wave equation}
$\partial_t^2u=-Lu$ describes vibrations of a membrane shaped like the surface. In both cases, the
eigenfunctions are the natural modes: expanding $u$ in the eigenbasis, each mode evolves
independently, decaying as $e^{-\lambda_kt}$ under heat diffusion, and oscillating with frequency
$\sqrt{\lambda_k}$ as a standing wave. The eigenvalues are the squared frequencies at which the surface
``sounds''. Kac's famous question, \emph{can one hear the shape of a drum?}, asks whether the
spectrum determines the shape. The answer is no: there are isospectral shapes that are not isometric.
In practice, however, such shapes are rare and the spectrum is a very discriminative signature.

\section{Eigenvalues and eigenfunctions}

The eigenpairs \eqref{eq:spec} have several properties that matter for shape analysis:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $\lambda_1=0$ with constant $\phi_1=1/\sqrt{A}$; the number of zero eigenvalues equals the
  number of connected components.
  \item \textbf{Isometry invariance}: isometric shapes have the same eigenvalues, and eigenfunctions
  that correspond through the isometry (up to sign and up to rotations within eigenspaces).
  \item \textbf{Scaling}: scaling the shape by $s$ scales every eigenvalue by $1/s^2$, so eigenvalues
  are normalised by the area, $\tilde\lambda_k=A\lambda_k$, or the mesh is rescaled to unit area.
  \item \textbf{Weyl's law} \citep{Weyl1911}: for a surface of area $A$,
  \begin{equation}
  \lambda_k\sim\frac{4\pi}{A}\,k\qquad(k\to\infty),
  \end{equation}
  so the spectrum grows linearly, and the deviation from this line carries the shape information.
  \item \textbf{Nodal domains}: $\phi_k$ has at most $k$ nodal domains (Courant's theorem), so higher
  eigenfunctions oscillate more.
  \item \textbf{Ambiguities}: each eigenfunction is defined up to sign, and eigenfunctions with equal
  or nearly equal eigenvalues can mix or swap order between two shapes.
\end{itemize}

\section{ShapeDNA}

\citet{Reuter2006} proposed the sequence of the first $N$ eigenvalues of the area-normalised
Laplace--Beltrami operator as a fingerprint, the \emph{ShapeDNA}:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{DNA}(S)=(\tilde\lambda_2,\tilde\lambda_3,\dots,\tilde\lambda_{N+1}) .
\end{equation}
It is isometry invariant, needs no alignment and no correspondence, and is compact. Shapes are
compared with a distance between the sequences; since the eigenvalues grow linearly, the differences
are divided by $k$ (or the eigenvalues normalised by Weyl's law) so that the high frequencies do not
dominate. \Cref{fig:shapedna} compares ShapeDNA with the extrinsic D2 shape distribution of
\cref{ch:shape} on five cats and five horses in different poses: with ShapeDNA every shape's nearest
neighbour has the right class (accuracy $1.0$), with D2 one cat is closest to a horse ($0.9$).

\fig{ch15_shapedna.png}{Left: normalised eigenvalues $A\lambda_k/(4\pi k)$ of five cats and five horses
in different poses; they approach Weyl's limit $1$ slowly, and the two classes form separate bands.
Centre and right: distance matrices between the ten shapes with the extrinsic D2 distribution and with
ShapeDNA (dark is similar). ShapeDNA shows a clean two-class block structure; D2 is affected by the
poses.}{fig:shapedna}

ShapeDNA also has limitations: the notebook shows what it does not see (isospectral shapes, and
local changes that barely affect the low spectrum) and what it sees too much (topological noise, such
as a small handle created by a scanning artefact, or a change of mesh connectivity between parts,
changes the spectrum strongly).

\section{Fourier analysis on manifolds}

On the circle, the Fourier basis $e^{\mathrm ikx}$ consists of eigenfunctions of the second derivative.
On a manifold the eigenfunctions of $L$ play the same role: any $f\in L^2(\M)$ expands as
\begin{equation}
f=\sum_k\hat f_k\phi_k,\qquad \hat f_k=\inner{f}{\phi_k}_\M=\int_\M f\phi_k\dd A\approx\bphi_k\T\mM\bfun ,
\end{equation}
with Parseval's identity $\int_\M f^2\dd A=\sum_k\hat f_k^2$. Truncating the expansion is low-pass
filtering (\cref{ch:smooth}), and since $\inner{Lf}{f}=\sum_k\lambda_k\hat f_k^2$, the eigenvalue
measures the ``frequency'' (the Dirichlet energy) of each mode. To compare bases of two shapes, the
notebook correlates their eigenfunctions through a known correspondence and fixes the signs: the
matrix of inner products is nearly diagonal for low frequencies, with small blocks where eigenvalues
are close. This matrix is the functional map of \cref{ch:fmaps}.

\section{Heat diffusion and the heat kernel}

\subsection{Heat diffusion}

The heat equation $\partial_tu=-Lu$ with initial condition $u(\cdot,0)=u_0$ has the solution
\begin{equation}
u(x,t)=\sum_ke^{-\lambda_kt}\inner{u_0}{\phi_k}\phi_k(x)=\int_\M h_t(x,y)\,u_0(y)\dd y ,
\end{equation}
computed in the eigenbasis by multiplying each Fourier coefficient by $e^{-\lambda_kt}$
(\cref{fig:heat}). The total heat $\int u$ is conserved, and as $t\to\infty$ the temperature becomes
uniform.

\fig{ch15_heat.png}{Heat diffusing from the tip of the tail of the cat. At $t=0$ the heat is an
indicator of a small ball; it spreads along the tail and then over the body. Each panel uses its
own colour range; truncation to 200 eigenfunctions is accurate for $t\ge0.002$.}{fig:heat}

\subsection{The heat kernel}

The \emph{heat kernel} is the fundamental solution, the temperature at $y$ at time $t$ when a unit
amount of heat is placed at $x$:
\begin{equation}
h_t(x,y)=\sum_{k\ge1}e^{-\lambda_kt}\phi_k(x)\phi_k(y) .
\end{equation}
It is symmetric, positive, conserves heat ($\int_\M h_t(x,y)\dd y=1$), satisfies the semigroup property
$h_{t+s}(x,z)=\int_\M h_t(x,y)h_s(y,z)\dd y$, and is invariant to isometries. Small times probe a small
neighbourhood of $x$ and large times the global shape (\cref{fig:heatkernel}); for $t\to\infty$,
$h_t(x,y)\to1/A$. In the discrete setting, $h_t(x,\cdot)=\mPhi e^{-t\mLambda}\mPhi\T\be_x$ with the
truncated eigenbasis, or $\exp(-t\mM^{-1}\mL)$ applied to a delta for exact results.

\fig[0.75\textwidth]{ch15_heat_kernel.png}{Heat kernels $h_t(x,\cdot)$ from a point on an ear (top)
and from a point on the back (bottom) at three times. Each panel uses its own colour range, from
light (low) to dark (high). Small times are local; at $t=0.2$ the heat has reached the whole
body.}{fig:heatkernel}

\section{Diffusion maps and distances}

The heat kernel defines a family of embeddings and distances \citep{Coifman2006}. The \emph{diffusion
map} at scale $t$ sends a point to the vector of its weighted eigenfunction values,
\begin{equation}
\Psi_t(x)=\bigl(e^{-\lambda_2t}\phi_2(x),\,e^{-\lambda_3t}\phi_3(x),\,\dots\bigr),
\end{equation}
and the \emph{diffusion distance} is the Euclidean distance in this space,
\begin{equation}
d_t^2(x,y)=\norm{\Psi_t(x)-\Psi_t(y)}^2=\sum_ke^{-2\lambda_kt}\bigl(\phi_k(x)-\phi_k(y)\bigr)^2
=h_{2t}(x,x)+h_{2t}(y,y)-2h_{2t}(x,y) .
\end{equation}
Two points are close in diffusion distance if heat diffuses easily between them, through many short
paths; the distance is robust to topological noise and small holes, which change geodesic distances
drastically. The scale $t$ chooses which features matter: at small $t$ the diffusion distance saturates
quickly away from the source, and at large $t$ only the coarse structure remains.

Changing the weights $e^{-\lambda t}$ for other functions $g(\lambda)$ gives other distances,
$d^2(x,y)=\sum_kg(\lambda_k)^2(\phi_k(x)-\phi_k(y))^2$:
\begin{itemize}
  \item the \emph{commute-time} distance, $g(\lambda)=1/\sqrt\lambda$, related to the expected time of a
  random walk to go from $x$ to $y$ and back; the corresponding embedding is the GPS below;
  \item the \emph{biharmonic} distance \citep{Lipman2010}, $g(\lambda)=1/\lambda$, which is smooth, has
  no free parameter, and behaves like the geodesic distance globally while being smooth near the
  source.
\end{itemize}

\fig{ch15_distances.png}{Distances from a point on an ear of the cat: geodesic distance (heat
method), diffusion distances at two scales, commute-time (GPS) and biharmonic distances. The Spearman
rank correlations with the geodesic distance are $0.89$ and $0.97$ for diffusion at $t=0.01$ and
$t=0.1$, $0.94$ for GPS and $0.99$ for the biharmonic distance.}{fig:distances}

\paragraph{The GPS embedding.} \citet{Rustamov2007} proposed the \emph{global point signature}
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{GPS}(x)=\Bigl(\frac{\phi_2(x)}{\sqrt{\lambda_2}},\frac{\phi_3(x)}{\sqrt{\lambda_3}},\dots\Bigr)
\end{equation}
as a canonical form of the shape: it is isometry invariant, so two poses of the same shape map to the
same point set up to the sign and mixing ambiguities, and the Euclidean distance between embedded
points is the commute-time distance (the GPS is the embedding of the Green's function of $L$). The
alternative canonical forms of \citet{Elad2003} embed geodesic distances with multidimensional scaling.

\fig{ch15_gps.png}{GPS embedding (first three coordinates) of two poses of the cat, coloured by the
$x$ coordinate of the standing pose. Both embeddings look alike, with the tail as the long branch
and the legs as shorter ones. Their relative difference is $20\%$, partly because some eigenvalues
change between the poses ($\lambda_4$ goes from $18.5$ to $21.7$).}{fig:gps}

\section{Local descriptors}

\subsection{The heat kernel signature}

\citet{Sun2009} restrict the heat kernel to the diagonal, the amount of heat that remains at $x$ after
time $t$:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{HKS}(x,t)=h_t(x,x)=\sum_ke^{-\lambda_kt}\phi_k(x)^2 .
\end{equation}
Sampled at a set of times, it gives a vector at every point that is
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{intrinsic}, hence invariant to isometric deformations;
  \item \textbf{multiscale}: small $t$ describes the local geometry, large $t$ the global position of the
  point in the shape;
  \item \textbf{informative}: under mild conditions the family $\{h_t(x,x)\}_{t>0}$ determines the
  whole heat kernel, hence the metric;
  \item \textbf{stable} under small perturbations of the shape.
\end{itemize}
The times are usually sampled logarithmically between $t_{\min}=4\ln10/\lambda_{\max}$ and
$t_{\max}=4\ln10/\lambda_2$, where $\lambda_{\max}$ is the largest computed eigenvalue, and the
signatures are normalised by the heat trace $\sum_ke^{-\lambda_kt}$ to remove the dependence on scale
(\cref{fig:hks}).

\fig{ch15_hks.png}{Logarithm of the HKS at four times on the two poses of the cat, with a common
colour range per column. At small times the extremities (paws, tail tip, ears) retain more heat than
the body, since heat escapes slowly from protruding regions of positive curvature; at large times
only the global position along the body matters. The median relative difference between the
poses is between $2\%$ and $8\%$ depending on $t$.}{fig:hks}

\paragraph{HKS and Gaussian curvature.} For small $t$ the heat kernel has the asymptotic expansion
\begin{equation}
h_t(x,x)=\frac{1}{4\pi t}+\frac{K(x)}{12\pi}+O(t),
\end{equation}
so the HKS at small times is an intrinsic, multiscale version of the Gaussian curvature. On a mesh,
``small'' must still be larger than the squared edge length: below that scale the discrete heat
does not leave the vertex and the HKS measures the vertex area instead (\cref{fig:hks-curv}).

\fig{ch15_hks_curvature.pdf}{HKS against Gaussian curvature on an ellipsoid with semi-axes
$1.3$, $1$, $0.6$. Left: exact $K$. Centre: Pearson correlation between the HKS and $K$ against $t/h^2$
(with $h$ the mean edge length). For $t\lesssim h^2$ the correlation is negative, because the HKS then
reflects the vertex areas; it peaks at $0.974$ around $t/h^2\approx11$ and decreases at large $t$, where
global effects dominate. Right: at the best $t$, $\mathrm{HKS}-1/(4\pi t)$ grows with $K$ with slope
$0.020$, close to the theoretical $1/(12\pi)=0.027$.}{fig:hks-curv}

\subsection{The wave kernel signature}

The HKS filters $e^{-t\lambda}$ are low-pass: every scale is dominated by the lowest frequencies, so the
descriptor localises features poorly. \citet{Aubry2011} replace diffusion by the evolution of a quantum
particle, governed by the Schr\"odinger equation $\mathrm i\,\partial_t\psi=L\psi$. If the particle has
an energy distribution $f_e(\lambda)$ around energy $e$, its average probability of being at $x$ is
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{WKS}(x,e)=\frac{\sum_k\phi_k(x)^2f_e(\lambda_k)^2}{\sum_kf_e(\lambda_k)^2},\qquad
f_e(\lambda)^2=\exp\Bigl(-\frac{(e-\log\lambda)^2}{2\sigma^2}\Bigr),
\end{equation}
with log-normal energy distributions, since the eigenvalues grow linearly and their logarithms are
more evenly spread. The energies $e$ are sampled uniformly in $[\log\lambda_2,\log\lambda_{\max}]$ and
$\sigma$ is a multiple of the spacing. The filters are \emph{band-pass}, so each component of the WKS
isolates a range of frequencies, giving better localisation and better matching, at the cost of
less robustness to large deformations (\cref{fig:wks,fig:matching}).

\fig{ch15_wks.png}{Top: the HKS filters $e^{-t\lambda}$ are low-pass, while the WKS filters
$f_e^2(\lambda)$ are band-pass in $\log\lambda$; the ticks mark the eigenvalues of the cat. Bottom:
logarithm of the WKS at four energies. Low energies behave like the global HKS; high energies reveal
fine, oscillating patterns.}{fig:wks}

\subsection{Matching with descriptors}

Since the descriptors are isometry invariant, corresponding points of two poses have similar
descriptors, and the nearest neighbour in descriptor space is a correspondence candidate. Since the
TOSCA meshes share their vertex ordering across poses, the quality of such matches can be measured by
the geodesic distance between the matched point and the true one (\cref{fig:matching}). Pointwise
descriptor matching is noisy, especially between symmetric parts (left and right legs have identical
descriptors), so it is used as a starting point for the functional maps of \cref{ch:fmaps}.

\fign{ch15_matching.pdf}{Correspondences between two poses of the cat by nearest neighbour in
descriptor space, for 300 random points. Fraction of points matched within a geodesic error (shapes of
unit area): the WKS (median error $0.084$) localises better than the HKS ($0.106$). Many errors come from
symmetric flips.}{fig:matching}

\begin{keypoints}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item The eigenpairs of $\mL\bphi=\lambda\mM\bphi$ are intrinsic, multiscale and cheap to compute;
  eigenvalues grow like $4\pi k/A$ (Weyl).
  \item ShapeDNA (normalised eigenvalues) is a pose-invariant global signature.
  \item The heat kernel $h_t(x,y)=\sum e^{-\lambda_kt}\phi_k(x)\phi_k(y)$ yields diffusion distances,
  and other spectral weights yield commute-time (GPS) and biharmonic distances.
  \item HKS $=h_t(x,x)$ is a multiscale, curvature-like descriptor (low-pass); the WKS uses band-pass
  filters in $\log\lambda$ and localises better.
\end{itemize}
\end{keypoints}

it.
\end{exercise}
