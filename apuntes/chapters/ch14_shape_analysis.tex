\chapter{Shape Analysis}\label{ch:shape}

Algorithms on images work well for many tasks, but 2D information is often not enough: an image
loses depth, depends on the viewpoint and confuses shape with appearance. With 3D sensors becoming
common, \emph{shape analysis} incorporates 3D data into the process: it extracts knowledge from the
geometry. This chapter defines the main tasks, then studies global descriptors for similarity and
retrieval, local descriptors and the bag-of-features approach for partial similarity, and symmetry
detection, with applications to cultural heritage from the work of the course's lecturer. It
follows Tema 12; spectral descriptors, which underlie several of these methods, are developed in
\cref{ch:spectral}.

\section{Tasks}

For a shape $O=(V,E)$, the main tasks of shape analysis can be written as functions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{saliency detection}, $f:V\to\R$, assigns to each vertex a value that indicates its
  importance (for example to place interest points, \cref{ch:global});
  \item \textbf{segmentation}, $f:V\to\mathcal L$, assigns to each vertex a label from a set
  $\mathcal L$, one per segment or part;
  \item \textbf{symmetry detection} finds non-trivial transformations $T$ with $T(O)\cong O$, which
  preserve the shape of the object;
  \item \textbf{characterisation}, $f:V\to\R^n$, assigns to each vertex an $n$-dimensional vector that
  describes the local geometry (a local descriptor);
  \item \textbf{similarity}, $f:\mathcal U\times\mathcal U\to\R$, measures how similar two objects of a
  universe $\mathcal U$ are; it underlies retrieval and classification.
\end{itemize}

\section{Global descriptors}

The \emph{global} approach transforms a 3D object into a numerical or symbolic representation,
usually a feature vector (sometimes a graph), and compares two objects through their
representations with a distance (L1, L2, $\chi^2$). The feature vector approach has been studied
extensively, and indexing structures make retrieval systems scalable to large collections. A
descriptor must be invariant to the transformations that should not change the similarity:
translation, scale and rotation for rigid objects, and isometric deformation for articulated ones.

\subsection{Pose normalisation}

Many descriptors are not rotation invariant, and the usual remedy is to bring every object to a
canonical pose first:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item translate the centre of mass of the surface to the origin;
  \item rotate the object so that the axes of largest spread of the surface (the principal
  components of the area-weighted covariance of its points) align with $x$, $y$ and $z$;
  \item scale it to a standard size.
\end{enumerate}
PCA determines the axes only up to sign and order: each eigenvector can be flipped, and axes with
similar variance can swap under small perturbations. Heuristics fix the signs (for example by the
sign of the third moment along each axis), but they fail on nearly symmetric objects
(\cref{fig:pose}). Pose normalisation therefore works for classes with low intra-class variability;
an alternative is to make the descriptor itself rotation invariant, for example with spherical
harmonic magnitudes \citep{Kazhdan2003}.

\fig[0.6\textwidth]{ch14_pose_normalization.png}{Pose normalisation by PCA. Left: an airplane in an
arbitrary orientation with its principal axes (red, green, blue in decreasing variance). Right: after
alignment the principal axes coincide with the coordinate axes. The third-moment rule chose signs
that put the airplane upside down, a typical failure of sign disambiguation.}{fig:pose}

\subsection{The depth-buffer descriptor}

Image-based descriptors convert a 3D object into a set of images and extract numerical information
from them. The \emph{depth-buffer} descriptor \citep{Vranic2004} renders six orthographic depth
images of the pose-normalised object, looking along $\pm x$, $\pm y$ and $\pm z$, applies the 2D
discrete Fourier transform to each image, and keeps the magnitudes of the lowest frequencies as the
feature vector (\cref{fig:depthbuffer}). Magnitudes are invariant to cyclic shifts and the low
frequencies are robust to small details. The problem of image-based descriptors is that the images
differ when the pose changes, hence the need for pose normalisation; the light field descriptor
\citep{Chen2003} instead compares silhouettes from the vertices of a dodecahedron, minimising over
the rotations of the dodecahedron.

\fig{ch14_depth_buffer.png}{Depth-buffer descriptor of the normalised airplane: six orthographic
depth images (brighter is closer to the viewer) and the logarithm of the magnitude of the 2D Fourier
transform of the $+z$ image. The low-frequency magnitudes form the descriptor.}{fig:depthbuffer}

\subsection{PANORAMA}

PANORAMA \citep{Papadakis2010} is also image-based and requires pose normalisation, but uses
cylindrical instead of rectangular projections. For a cylinder aligned with a principal axis, it
casts rays from the axis outwards at angles $\theta$ and heights $z$, and records the distance of the
outermost intersection with the surface, $s(\theta,z)$ (and, in a second image, the orientation of
the normal there). The result is a panoramic image of the whole object, computed for the three
principal axes, and the descriptor is formed by the magnitudes of its 2D Fourier coefficients and by
statistics of its wavelet coefficients (\cref{fig:panorama}). The cylindrical projection captures the
whole object in each image, including parts hidden in orthographic views.

\fig{ch14_panorama.png}{PANORAMA for a horse. Left: the cylinder axis is the first principal axis
(dashed). Centre: the panoramic image $s(\theta,z)$; bright values are surface points far from the
axis (legs, neck and head), dark values points close to it. Right: low-frequency magnitudes of its 2D
Fourier transform.}{fig:panorama}

\subsection{Shape distributions}

Statistical descriptors avoid alignment altogether. The \emph{D2 shape distribution}
\citep{Osada2002} is the histogram of the Euclidean distances between pairs of random points on the
surface, normalised by the mean distance. It is invariant to rigid motions and scale, cheap and
robust to noise and tessellation. Like all descriptors based on Euclidean distances it is
\emph{extrinsic}: it changes when an articulated object changes pose. \Cref{fig:shapedna} in the next
chapter compares it with the intrinsic ShapeDNA on cats and horses in several poses: with D2 the
nearest neighbour of one of the cat poses is a horse, while the intrinsic descriptor classifies every
shape correctly.

\subsection{Evaluating retrieval}

A retrieval system ranks the collection by similarity to a query. With class labels as ground truth,
it is evaluated by precision--recall curves, the \emph{nearest neighbour} rate (the fraction of
queries whose closest match is of the same class), the \emph{first} and \emph{second tier} (the
fraction of the class retrieved in the first $C-1$ and $2(C-1)$ results, for a class of size $C$), and
the discounted cumulative gain, which rewards correct results near the top. The Princeton Shape
Benchmark \citep{Shilane2004} and the SHREC contests provide standard collections.

\section{Partial similarity and local descriptors}

\emph{Partial similarity} arises in two situations. In the first, objects share similar parts, whose
extent is not known a priori (two chairs with the same legs, or a fragment and the vase it belongs
to). In the second, the query is a single view of an object, as captured by a scanner, and we want the
models with similar views. In both cases local information is better than global information to
characterise portions of a shape, so a local analysis of the geometry is required: a local descriptor
at many points (spin images, FPFH, the heat kernel signature of \cref{ch:spectral}), and a way of
aggregating them.

\subsection{Bag of features}

The \emph{bag of features} approach comes from text retrieval, where documents are represented by
the frequencies of their words irrespective of order. The distribution of local descriptors in feature
space reveals local structures useful for retrieval:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item compute local descriptors at many points of every shape in a training collection;
  \item cluster them (for example with $k$-means) into a \emph{vocabulary} of $K$ geometric words, the
  cluster centres;
  \item for a shape, assign each descriptor to its nearest word (vector quantisation) or softly to
  several words with weights, and accumulate the histogram of word frequencies, weighted by the area of
  each point;
  \item compare shapes by a distance between histograms, optionally with tf-idf weights that
  downweight words common to all shapes.
\end{enumerate}
The histogram discards where the words occur, which gives invariance to articulation and robustness
to partiality and clutter (\cref{fig:bof}).

\fig{ch14_bof.png}{Bag of features with a vocabulary of 12 words built by $k$-means on heat kernel
signatures of a cat and a horse. Each point is coloured by its word: the vocabulary separates
extremities (paws, tail tip, ears), limbs and the torso. The area-weighted word histograms are the
global descriptors; their L1 distance is $0.449$.}{fig:bof}

Shape Google \citep{Bronstein2011} builds the vocabulary from heat kernel signatures, which are
invariant to isometric deformations, adds \emph{spatially sensitive} bags of features (histograms of
pairs of words weighted by their heat kernel proximity) to recover some geometric layout, and
compresses the vectors into binary codes for fast search. A more recent alternative replaces vector
quantisation by \emph{Fisher vectors} \citep{Perronnin2010}, which encode the deviation of the local
descriptors (for example FPFH) from a Gaussian mixture model; this generalises the counting of words
to first- and second-order statistics.

\section{Symmetry detection}

Many objects are symmetric, and detecting symmetries helps to understand their structure and
regularity, to compress, to segment, and to complete missing parts.

\paragraph{Extrinsic symmetries.} A reflection or rotation $T$ with $T(O)\cong O$ maps points to points
with the same local geometry. Transformation-space voting \citep{Mitra2006} samples points, pairs those
with similar local descriptors (for example principal curvatures), lets every pair vote for the
transformation that maps one point to the other (for a reflection, the bisecting plane), and finds
clusters in transformation space; each cluster is a candidate symmetry, possibly partial, which is then
verified and grown on the surface.

\paragraph{Descriptors from heat diffusion.} Heat diffusion on a surface from point sources is
governed by the heat equation, and its solution, the heat kernel, depends on the eigenvalues and
eigenfunctions of the Laplace--Beltrami operator (\cref{ch:spectral}). Because the operator is
intrinsic, a variation of the solution of the heat equation guarantees that symmetric points have
similar characterisations \citep{Sun2009}. \Citet{Sipiran2014} use this to detect approximate
reflective symmetries in \emph{partial} meshes: detect local maxima of a diffusion-based function,
match points with similar descriptors, and use the reflective symmetries they induce as hypotheses that
are verified on the surface. Intrinsic symmetries, which map the surface to itself preserving geodesic
distances (the left and right sides of a person in any pose), appear as sign changes of the
Laplace--Beltrami eigenfunctions \citep{Ovsjanikov2008}.

\paragraph{Cultural heritage.} Archaeological objects are often broken or eroded, and symmetry is a
powerful prior to restore them. Partial symmetries let the missing geometry be synthesised from the
symmetric counterpart, as in object completion by $k$-sparse optimisation \citep{Mavridis2015}.
Axial symmetry is especially common: pottery is made on a wheel. \Citet{Sipiran2017} uses diffusion
descriptors to find evidence of partial axial symmetry with a pipeline of four steps: sample points,
find \emph{supporting circles} (sets of points with similar descriptors lying on circles), detect the
axis consistent with most circles, and complete the object by rotating its profile around the axis.
Data-driven methods go further and learn the structure of object classes with deep generative models to
complete shapes.

\begin{keypoints}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item Shape analysis tasks: saliency, segmentation, symmetry, characterisation and similarity.
  \item Global descriptors (depth buffer, PANORAMA) usually require PCA pose normalisation, which is
  ambiguous in sign and order; shape distributions avoid it but are extrinsic.
  \item Partial similarity needs local descriptors aggregated with bags of features (vocabulary by
  $k$-means, word histograms) or Fisher vectors.
  \item Symmetry detection uses transformation voting or diffusion-based descriptors, with
  applications to the restoration of cultural heritage objects.
\end{itemize}
\end{keypoints}

\section*{Exercises}
\begin{exercise}
Show that the eigenvectors of the covariance matrix of a shape rotate with the shape, and that the
third moments along them change sign when the shape is reflected. Why does this make pose
normalisation ambiguous for symmetric shapes?
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}
Prove that the magnitude of the 2D discrete Fourier transform is invariant to cyclic shifts of the
image. Which rotations of the object does this make PANORAMA robust to?
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}
In a bag of features with $K$ words built from $D$-dimensional descriptors, how many numbers does a
Fisher vector with a $K$-component diagonal Gaussian mixture store? Compare with the word histogram.
\end{exercise}
